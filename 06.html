01 / Overview

Give the audience the reason first.

A strong project conversation does not begin with a list of tools. It begins with the need, the people affected, and the outcome you wanted to create.

The challenge is simple to state. The solution is not.

The project started from a limitation I encountered with conventional 3D printers: The available print volume was not large enough for the type of mechanical prototypes I wanted to develop.
As a Robotics Engineering student working on mechanical and mechatronic projects, I wanted to be able to prototype larger mechanical components and assemblies, particularly parts related to robotic arms and other large scale mechanisms.
Instead of adapting my designs to fit a small printer, I decided to approach the problem from the other direction: What if I designed and built a 3D printer around the size of prototypes I actually wanted to manufacture?
This became the reason for developing a large-format 3D printer.

01 / Need

What needed solving?

The problem was not simply: "I need a 3D printer."
It was: "I need a 3D printer with a sufficiently large build volume to manufacture the prototypes I want to design."
A larger printer, however, creates new engineering problems. Increasing the size of the printer also increases:
- Structural loads.
- Bending.
- Deflection.
- Motor torque requirements.
- Lead-screw loading.
- Buckling risk.
- Shaft loading.
- Frame instability.
- Moving mass.
- Manufacturing requirements.
So the project became more than a 3D-printer assembly project. It became a structural and mechanical engineering design problem

02 / Decision

Why this approach?

Before purchasing or fabricating components, I created a rough hand sketch and 3d draft of the proposed printer.
The purpose of those sketches were to establish:
- Approximate overall dimensions.
- General frame arrangement.
- Motion axes.
- Position of the Z-axis.
- Lead-screw arrangement.
- Motor locations.
- Supporting pillars.
- Moving modules.
- General mechanism.
This was the first transition from: "I want a large printer." to: "This is how I think a large printer could physically work." The sketch then became the basis for the engineering calculations.

04 / Process

From problem to working system.

Document the engineering process in the order it actually happened: define the problem, establish requirements, develop and analyse concepts, build prototypes, test against measurable criteria, iterate from evidence, and record the final outcome.

01

Problem definition

The main objective was defined as: Design and build a large-format 3D printer capable of producing large prototypes while maintaining sufficient structural strength, positional stability, and reliable axis movement.
The design therefore needed to satisfy two requirements simultaneously:
Large
Large enough for the intended robotic and mechanical prototypes.
Stable
Rigid enough that increasing the size would not result in excessive:
- Deflection.
- Vibration.
- Misalignment.
- Buckling.
- Shaft deformation.
- Positioning errors.
This created the fundamental engineering trade-off: The larger the printer becomes, the more carefully the structure and motion systems have to be designed.

02

Requirements and constraints

The requirements included:
- Large build volume.
- Rigid frame.
- Stable Z-axis.
- Reliable X/Y movement.
- Controlled Z-axis movement.
- Sufficient motor torque.
- Adequate shaft strength.
- Adequate lead-screw strength.
- Resistance to buckling.
- Controlled deflection.
- Reliable bearings.
- Manufacturability.
- Reasonable safety margin.
A factor of safety of 2.5 was used as an important design criterion for the structural calculations.
This meant the components were not designed simply to survive the expected load. They were designed with additional capacity to account for uncertainties and real-world conditions.

03

Research and investigation

The next step was deciding how the printer should actually move. Several mechanical considerations were investigated, particularly for the Z axis.
A lead-screw-based mechanism was selected because it provides: - Controlled linear movement.
- High mechanical advantage.
- Good load carrying capability.
- Suitable positioning.
- Resistance to back-driving depending on the screw geometry.
- Practical implementation for a large machine.
For the screw mechanism, an ACME screw was selected. The reason was not simply availability. The screw was evaluated from an engineering perspective, including:
- Pitch.
- Lead.
- Torque.
- Friction.
- Load capacity.
- Self-locking/back-driving behavior.
- Strength.
- Manufacturing practicality.
The higher friction of the ACME form can be useful in a loaded linear mechanism because it can improve resistance to unwanted movement

The interaction between these systems was considered during the design. That last point is important. The components were not analyzed in isolation. The effect of one component on the rest of the structure was considered.

05

Detailed design & mechanical calculations

This was one of the biggest parts of the project. Rather than selecting shafts and motors based only on what was available, the required dimensions and capacities were calculated.
Z-Axis Structural Study
The Z-axis was studied extensively because it carries significant loads and directly affects the stability of the printer. The analysis included:
Shear force : The applied loads were used to determine the shear forces acting on the structural members.
Bending moment : The bending moment was calculated to determine how strongly the members would tend to deform under load.
Bearing loading : The forces transmitted through the bearings were also considered.
Shaft sizing : The shaft diameter was calculated based on the required strength and stability. After evaluating the different possibilities, a 12 mm shaft diameter was selected.
The resulting calculated factor of safety was approximately: FoS = 3.39
This was above the target factor of safety of 2.5. That provided additional margin for the selected shaft under the evaluated loading conditions

06

Bending and deflection

For a large-format printer, strength alone is not enough. A component can technically be strong enough not to break but still deform enough to affect print quality.
Therefore, the analysis considered:
- Bending moment.
- Deflection.
- Support conditions.
- Load distribution.
- Interaction between components.
This is particularly important for a large printer because even a relatively small structural deflection can translate into positioning errors over a large build volume.
So the design question became: Will the structure remain sufficiently rigid, not simply "will it break?

07

Buckling Analysis

The vertical members and other compression-loaded components were also checked for buckling.
This was important because increasing the height of the printer increases the possibility of instability in slender structural members.
The analysis considered the relationship between:
- Applied compressive load.
- Member length.
- Cross-sectional properties.
- Material properties.
- End/support conditions.
The goal was to ensure that the structural members would not experience instability before reaching their required working loads.

08

Motor torque calculation

The motors were not selected randomly. The required torque was calculated from the mechanical loads acting on the system.
For a rotational system: T =Fr
where:
- T= torque
- F= force
- r= effective radius.
For the lead-screw system, the torque requirement also depends on:
- Axial load.
- Screw lead.
- Screw efficiency.
- Friction.
A simplified relationship can be represented as: T ∝ FL/η
where:
- F= axial load
- L= lead
- η= screw efficiency.
This allowed the motor requirement to be connected directly to the mechanical requirements of the printer

09

Lead-screw study

The lead-screw system received its own engineering study.
We considered:
- Screw diameter.
- Pitch.
- Lead.
- Axial loading.
- Torque.
- Friction.
- Locking behavior.
- Buckling.
- Strength.
- Motor requirements.
The purpose was to make sure the lead screw could both move the required load and remain structurally safe.
The ACME screw mechanism was selected after considering these factors. This was particularly important because the lead screw is not just a transmission component—it is also part of the structural load path.

10

Arm/moving mechanism study

The moving mechanism was also analyzed to determine:
- Number of motors required.
- Required motor torque.
- Applied loads.
- Mechanical moments.
- Load distribution.
- Interaction between the moving components.
The mechanism was therefore sized based on the actual loads rather than simply choosing motors based on their availability.
This helped answer: How many motors do I actually need, and what torque must each motor provide?

11

Interaction between components

One of the strongest aspects of this project was that the analysis did not stop at individual components.
For example: Motor → lead screw → bearing → shaft → Z-axis module → frame
Every component transfers forces to another component. Therefore, changing one component can change the loading of another.
For example:
- Increasing the printer size increases the load.
- Increased load affects the lead screw.
- Lead-screw loading affects motor torque.
- Motor and screw forces affect the bearings.
- Bearing forces affect the supporting structure.
- Structural flexibility affects printer accuracy.
This creates a complete mechanical chain.

12

Prototype & fabrication

Once the mechanical design and calculations were established, the printer could move toward fabrication.
The important difference here was that the prototype was not simply assembled from commercially available components. The structure was developed around the calculated requirements of the intended machine. This included consideration of:
- Frame dimensions.
- Shaft diameter.
- Lead-screw mechanism.
- Motor requirements.
- Bearing arrangement.
- Structural supports.
- Assembly interfaces

Right now I am at the fabrication process. With all the calculation done I have a good amount of confidence on the safety and integrity of the design.

14

Integration & System testing

After the individual systems were tested, everything could be integrated. The complete sequence becomes: Command → Motor → Mechanical transmission → Axis movement → Position → Printing

The final test is not simply: "Does the printer move?"
It is: "Can the entire system maintain accurate and stable movement while carrying out an actual print?

15

Failure Analysis & Iteration

The project was designed with failure prevention in mind.
Instead of waiting for the physical prototype to fail, potential failure modes were considered mathematically.

These included:
- Excessive bending.
- Shaft failure.
- Bearing overload.
- Lead-screw failure.
- Buckling.
- Insufficient motor torque.
- Structural instability.
- Excessive deflection.
- Misalignment.
The factor of safety of 2.5 was incorporated into the design process to provide additional capacity beyond the expected working loads. The 12 mm shaft selection, for example, resulted in a calculated factor of safety of 3.39, giving additional margin relative to the original target. This demonstrates a key engineering principle: Failure analysis should happen before failure happens

16

Validation & Final Evalution

The final printer should ultimately be evaluated against the original purpose: - Can it provide the required large build volume? - Can the structure remain stable? - Can the motors move the required loads? - Can the lead screws support and accurately position the Z axis? - Can the structure resist bending and buckling? - Can the machine maintain sufficient positioning accuracy? - Can it successfully produce large prototypes? These questions connect the final machine directly back to the reason the project started

17

Engineering Lessons

I have not finish the printer yet, thus I can not draw the final lessons learnt. However so far I can say that starting a project with an estimation of what could go wrong and right, and being able to quantity both gives some kind of motivational push that make me want to see the outcome.
I have to finish this project to know how much I can trust the numbers and how I could have made more accurate estimations, and most improtantly what I missed in my estimation to ensure that those parameters do not get overlooked in my futur endeavors.

18

Documentation and next iteration

The documentation should preserve the entire engineering journey:
Problem → Rough Sketch → Requirements → Research → Concept → Mechanical Architecture → Calculations → Shaft Study → Z-Axis Study → Lead-Screw Study → Motor Torque → Buckling → Factor of Safety → CAD → Fabrication → Testing → Failure Analysis → Improvements → Final Printer
And the next iteration can focus on things such as:
- Increasing build volume further if required.
- Improving rigidity.
- Reducing vibration.
- Improving Z-axis synchronization.
- Improving print accuracy.
- Increasing printing speed.
- Improving thermal management.
- Improving electronics.
- Adding better monitoring and safety features.
- Making the printer easier to maintain.

The complete story in one line: The 3D printer project started from a personal need for a larger prototyping platform, then evolved into a complete mechanical engineering study where the required machine size drove the structural design, shaft sizing, bearing loads, motor torque, lead screw selection, buckling analysis, and factor-of-safety calculations before the physical machine was built
