01 / Overview

Give the audience the reason first.

A strong project conversation does not begin with a list of tools. It begins with the need, the people affected, and the outcome you wanted to create.

The challenge is simple to state. The solution is not.

The project began from the idea of creating a mobile robotic platform capable of transporting items from one location to another with minimal human involvement.
The long-term vision is a rover that could operate within a local area and transport items between people—for example, one person could place an item on the rover, provide its destination, and the rover could deliver it to another person.
The concept is intended to evolve beyond simple remote control.
The long-term system could combine:
- Remote control.
- Semi-autonomous operation.
- Autonomous navigation.
- Long-range communication.
- Outdoor mobility.
- Payload transportation.
- Additional robotic attachments.
The idea is therefore not simply to build a remote-controlled car. The goal is to develop a robotic transportation platform that can eventually operate in real-world environments and perform useful tasks

03 / The story

01 / Need

What needed solving?

The initial question was:
How can we create a rover that can transport objects and eventually operate beyond direct line-of-sight control?
A conventional RC vehicle is limited because the operator needs to remain relatively close to the vehicle.
For a transportation robot, that is not enough. The rover needs a communication system that allows a user to send commands from a much greater distance.
This led to the first major technical direction: Internet-based control using MQTT

02 / Decision

Why this approach?

MQTT was selected because it provides a lightweight communication method suitable for exchanging commands and sensor information between the rover and a remote interface.
Instead of: Remote → Direct radio → Rover
the system became: Web interface → Internet → MQTT broker → Rover
This opened the possibility of controlling the rover from essentially anywhere with network connectivity. It also creates a foundation for eventually moving from manual control toward more intelligent and autonomous behavior.

04 / Process

From problem to working system.

Document the engineering process in the order it actually happened: define the problem, establish requirements, develop and analyse concepts, build prototypes, test against measurable criteria, iterate from evidence, and record the final outcome.

01

Stage 1 - Small Prototype

The first stage focused on proving the communication and control concept before building the larger physical platform. An ESP32 was used as the main controller.
The prototype included:
- ESP32.
- Motor driver.
- DC motors.
- Rover chassis.
- Sensors.
- Power system.
- MQTT communication.
- Web-based control interface.
The purpose of this stage was not to create the final transport rover.
It was to answer: Can we reliably communicate with and control a physical rover remotely through the internet?

02

Problem During Stage 1 — Power Management

One of the major problems encountered during development was power management. Two ESP32 boards were damaged during the development process because the power system was not sufficiently protected from disturbances generated by the motor/driver system. This was an important engineering lesson. Motors are not electrically "quiet" loads.
Their switching and changing current demand can introduce:
- Voltage fluctuations.
- Electrical noise.
- Transient effects.
- Back EMF-related disturbances.
These disturbances can affect sensitive electronics. Instead of simply replacing the ESP32 and continuing, the power system was investigated and modified

03

Failure analysis and solution

The power-management problem led to a redesign of the protection around the control electronics.
The system was improved using components such as:
- Capacitors for filtering and stabilizing the supply.
- Diodes for protection against unwanted voltage transients/back EMF paths.
- Better separation/management of the motor and logic power paths.
The goal was to prevent motor-related electrical disturbances from reaching and damaging the ESP32. This created one of the strongest engineering stories in the rover project: The system failed → the failure was investigated → the electrical architecture was modified → the system was retested.
The lesson was that mechanical and electrical systems cannot be treated independently

04

05

Local Testing

The first communication tests were performed locally. The rover was successfully controlled through the MQTT system.
This established that:
- The ESP32 could connect to the broker.
- Commands could be published.
- The rover could receive commands.
- The motor controller could interpret those commands.
- The rover could respond accordingly.
This was an important proof-of-concept milestone

06

Moving From Local to Web Control

The next step was extending the system beyond local operation. The rover was connected to a web-based interface.
The user could interact with controls such as:
- Forward.
- Reverse.
- Left.
- Right.
- Stop.
The commands were sent through MQTT to the rover.
The major achievement of Stage 1 was therefore: The rover could be controlled remotely through the web rather than only through a local connection.
This established the communication foundation for the larger rover.

08

Stage 2 — Larger Outdoor Rover

The project is now moving into its second stage. The focus is shifting from: "Can we remotely control a rover?" to: "Can we build a rover capable of carrying useful payloads and operating in a real outdoor environment?"
This requires a major mechanical redesign.

09

New Mechanical Requirements

The second prototype will have:
- Larger frame.
- Larger body.
- Stronger wheels.
- Greater payload capacity.
- More suitable outdoor mobility.
- More robust mechanical components.
- Improved steering.
- More space for electronics.
- Space for future attachments.
The design therefore has to move from a small experimental rover toward a functional robotic platform.

10

Steering System

One of the major changes between Stage 1 and Stage 2 is the steering method. The first prototype used a form of differential/friction steering, where the rover could turn by changing the direction or speed of wheels on different sides.
For example:
Left side → forward
Right side → reverse
This allows the rover to rotate around its own center. That is useful for compact robots, but it is not necessarily the ideal approach for the larger outdoor platform.

Stage 2 Steering
The new rover is being designed around a car-like steering mechanism. Instead of rotating the rover by driving opposite sides in opposite directions, the wheels will be steered mechanically. Conceptually:
Steering input
↓
Steering mechanism
↓
Wheel angle changes
↓
Rover changes direction
This should provide behavior more similar to a conventional vehicle and is more appropriate for the intended outdoor platform

14

Future Development

The larger rover is intended to become more than a remotely controlled vehicle. Future stages could introduce:

Semi-autonomous operation
The operator gives the rover a destination or task, while the system handles some of the movement.

Autonomous navigation
The rover could eventually:
- Determine its position.
- Detect obstacles.
- Plan a route.
- Navigate toward a destination.
- Stop when necessary.
- Return to a defined location.
Payload system
The rover could eventually have:
- Storage compartments.
- Delivery trays.
- Robotic arms.
- Lifting mechanisms.
- Specialized attachments.
This would transform the rover from a transportation platform into a multi purpose robotic platform

15

Engineering lessons

The rover project has already demonstrated several important engineering lessons.

1. Start with the bigger problem
The final goal wasn't simply: "Build a rover." It was: "Build a robotic platform capable of performing useful transportation tasks."
That changes how every subsystem is designed.
2. Prototype before scaling
Stage 1 allowed the communication architecture to be proven before investing heavily in the larger mechanical platform.
3. Failure is information
The damaged ESP32 boards exposed a weakness in the power architecture. Instead of hiding the failure, the project used it to improve the system.
4. Mechanical and electrical engineering are connected
A motor is not only a mechanical device. Its electrical behavior can directly affect the controller.
5. Build in stages
The project naturally follows:
